# Baby monitors

Most purchase decisions follow a pattern: they're driven by how well a product matches stated needs, its price, and how easy it is to get. But this framework breaks down fast. 

1. Needs are non-exhaustive, not static, and not explicit. And they can be influenced by other people or external events. And some needs are "must have" and some are "nice-to-have". Oops. 
2. Needs are of two types: Functional and quantifiable ("I need a glass of water NOW", "I DO NOT want sugar in my cocktail"). In this case it is easy to supply the feature because there is a binary outcome on whether you can meet the need or not. But equally often needs are not functional or quantifiable e.g. "status" or "risk aversion" are perfectly acceptable needs and the basis of whole industries (luxury goods, many services you buy). And don't forget these can be must have or nice to have (you can make a "2x2" if you're into that). More complexity? Most purchase decisions involve a mix of functional and quantifiable needs and non-functional / quantifiable needs. (today often called "vibes"). In fact, many companies try to either inject "vibes" as a need in the purchase making decision of the consumer, often because every competitor can meet the functional needs but fewer can meet the non-functional needs. This is known as branding.   
3. If you don't actually buy a product, the purchase decision looks incomprehensible. This is why startup advice always says "build something you want"—reverse-engineering someone else's needs is exhausting. 
4. Companies also wield price and availability as levers. A cheap product (Amazon, cloud credits) or omnipresent one (Coca-Cola in every vending machine, CoPilot in every IDE) wins not just features. But these are separate forces from the feature-needs match itself.
5. But here's the flip side: sometimes customers *gladly* pay more and wait longer because the feature-needs fit is just that good. See Hermes Birkin bags, Ferraris. This brings us to the Motorola AM21 baby monitor. I just bought one, and it's a masterclass in feature-needs matching—and it achieves this by doing *less*, not more (hello margins). No screen (good: parents get a break from constant surveillance). No camera (good: babies deserve privacy, and yes, it's unhackable). Three buttons: power, volume up, volume down. That's it. No app. No setup beyond plugging it in. No wifi. Parents are tired—they want things that work, not "smart" things that require a PhD or apps that look like cockputs. The AM21 costs INR 5,000 for what's essentially 50-year-old technology (probably costs INR 50 to manufacture). It takes 15 days to ship. And none of that matters.
